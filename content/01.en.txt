Ravel’s Violin Sonata No. 1 takes its name, “Violin Sonata No. 1, M. 12,” from the musicologist Marcel Marnat, who numbered Ravel’s works in the order they were composed. For a long time, Ravel was believed to have written only one violin sonata. Then a violin sonata from his student years was discovered, and it was published in 1975, 38 years after his death. Since it was composed in 1897, the piece had lain forgotten for nearly 80 years. That is the work we now know as “Violin Sonata No. 1, M. 12.”

The sonata long thought to be his only one had in fact been written about 25 years after this first one. So the familiar sonata became “Violin Sonata No. 2,” and the newly found work, by its date of composition, became “Violin Sonata No. 1.” It was most likely an assignment for Gabriel Fauré’s class while Ravel was a student, and it is thought to have been premiered by George Enescu on violin with Ravel himself at the piano.

We cannot know whether it was planned as a single movement from the start or as a work in three or four movements. Even so, this one published movement shows how Ravel understood sonata form and how he began trying new things within it. His impressionistic sense of colour is usually traced to the piano piece Jeux d’eau (1901), but the Violin Sonata No. 1 shows that the tendency was already present in his earlier work. At a time when sonata form was seen as old-fashioned, Ravel seems to have tried dressing it in his own grammar and colours.

In a piece with an introduction, the first theme usually arrives only after the opening passage. Here, the violin presents the first theme right in the introduction. It is played again with the piano, so by the point where the structure would normally introduce it, the first theme is already appearing for the third time. Its opening fragment serves as a bass line in the piano and as the bridge before the second theme. The development begins with the same melody, fragments of it carry the climax into the recapitulation, and in the coda it returns slowly (Lent), then more slowly still (encore plus lent), to close the piece.

Alongside this classical technique of developing a melody, Ravel also tries something new. One hallmark of Impressionist painting was blending colours to soften lines and outlines, and Ravel seems to blur the beat, which acts as the vertical lines of music. The piece not only opens in an unusual 7/8 metre but begins with triplets, which unsettles the sense of pulse. In some passages the violin and piano carry two time signatures at once, 6/8 and 2/4; in others the right and left hands are written in different metres. One passage is clearly marked 6/8 yet is meant to sound like 3/4.

Had this work never been found, Maurice Ravel’s reputation would be no different from what it is today. Still, student works like this one are valuable for tracing how his writing changed as his music matured. Just as archaeologists rejoice at a new find, one can imagine how intrigued performers must have been when this score was discovered and published.
