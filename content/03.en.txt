Many of the instruments we see in concert halls today did not exist in their present form from the start. They have been improved and refined over time, and they stay alive through the works composers write for them. Along the way, some instruments fell out of use, and some disappeared altogether. To survive, and to remain an instrument that can carry a solo career, an instrument needs enough repertoire, and as a tool for making sound, its strengths must outweigh its weaknesses.

The harp is a very old instrument, but for a long time it had a serious limitation. Its strings are arranged in groups of seven, from one red string to the next. Each string, when plucked, sounds a note corresponding to a white key on the piano. The problem was the notes of the black keys. These had to be played using a separate mechanism, which could not switch quickly and made changing keys difficult. This was a major weakness: it shrank the list of pieces the harp could play and the role it could take in an orchestra or alongside other instruments.

Because of this weakness, the harp was steadily improved through the Classical and Romantic eras. Then Ravel, a promising young composer at the time, received a commission. The instrument maker Érard had completed a harp built with the newest technology and asked for a piece featuring it. The harp was equipped with a new mechanism called the “double action,” which overcame the old limitation. Only then did the harp become a “complete instrument” that could move freely through every key, and Érard asked Ravel for a piece in which the harp could take the leading role.

Ravel himself seems not to have been very interested. He was dejected after failing to win the Prix de Rome, and with a deadline looming while he prepared for a trip with friends, he is said to have written it in a hurry over three sleepless nights. The piece was even left out of the list of works he later compiled.

Whatever his lukewarm attitude, the piece has remained one of the most important works in the harp repertoire. Its scoring for seven players, harp with string quartet, flute and clarinet, is unusual, and the piece itself, with melodies and figures far freer and more colourful than in his String Quartet, captures Impressionist music well. As the title says, it is an introduction and allegro aiming at sonata form, and as in the Violin Sonata No. 1, a melody from the introduction plays an important role in the allegro.

The piece can also be played almost like a harp concerto. A letter survives saying that with the strings doubled or tripled and the solo lines well divided, it sounds even better than in its original scoring. Ravel himself arranged it for two pianos and for harp and piano.

After the premiere, Ravel probably came to value the harp’s possibilities and versatility. He included the harp in every one of his orchestral works, sometimes giving it an important solo role. When he orchestrated Tzigane, originally for violin and piano, he gave the arpeggio figures to the harp, creating a more mysterious atmosphere than the piano’s. And in the first movement of his Piano Concerto in G major, he reused an idea from the harp cadenza of Introduction and Allegro: the right hand sweeping back and forth in glissandos while the left plays the melody in harmonics.

Meanwhile, another maker, Pleyel, built a harp that could play chromatic notes without pedals and commissioned a piece from Debussy. The result was Danse sacrée et danse profane, which today is played on the Érard-style double-action pedal harp. Pleyel’s design added more strings so that the black-key notes could be played directly, but it was too complicated to play and hard to tune, and it faded into history.
