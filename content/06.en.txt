Imagine someone with the surname Sa and the lovely given name Bada, the native Korean word for “sea.” Composer Sa Bada wants to write a piece that will leave his name behind. If we write out the notes of C major with English and Korean letter names, la-ti-do-re-mi-fa-sol becomes A-B-C-D-E-F-G in English and 가-나-다-라-마-바-사 (ga-na-da-ra-ma-ba-sa) in Korean. So the composer “Sa-Ba-Da” becomes sol-fa-do in solfège, or G-F-C in English letter names. (Counting in order, the three syllables sit in seventh, sixth and third place. I hope that makes sense.)

There really is a compositional technique that works this way: the musical cryptogram. It was first used by Bach, fittingly for the father of music, in the third subject of the unfinished Contrapunctus 14 from his last work, The Art of Fugue (Die Kunst der Fuge, BWV 1080). In German, B-flat is called B and B-natural is called H, so Bach was able to inscribe his name as a four-note motif, B-A-C-H: B-flat, A, C, B.

Later, the late Romantic composer Liszt used this four-note motif in his Fantasy and Fugue on the Theme B-A-C-H as an homage to Bach. Cryptograms that write a name into notes can be found throughout the history of Western music.

Shostakovich is a well-known example of a composer using his own name. From D. Schostakowitsch, the letters D, S, C, H become the notes D, E-flat (Es in German), C and B. This four-note motif appears across many of his works, including string quartets, symphonies and a violin concerto. Holst’s orchestral suite The Planets also opens “Uranus” with a cryptogram motif based on the composer’s own name.

Carl Philipp Emanuel Bach, the best known of Bach’s composer sons, also used the technique, but the piece went unpublished and the technique was forgotten for a while. The composer who revived it was Robert Schumann. His Op. 1, the Abegg Variations, is built on a cryptogram: the theme begins A-B-flat-E-G-G, spelling Abegg, the surname of a friend, possibly an imaginary one. It was the first time someone else’s name, not the composer’s own, was used this way. Later, in his piano cycle Carnaval, he wove in the name of his sweetheart’s hometown as a cryptogram, and he went on to write Six Fugues on B-A-C-H. Brahms, Grieg and Elgar also left traces of the technique.

The musical cryptogram is not widely used, but it captures a composer’s intention more clearly than any other technique, with no room for misunderstanding. Ravel used it to express his respect for his teacher.

Earlier, in 1909, a call went out for pieces on a theme spelling Haydn’s name in notes to mark the centenary of his death, and Ravel first used the technique in the minuet he wrote for it. In 1922, La Revue musicale published a special tribute issue for Fauré and commissioned several composers to write pieces with the same technique. Ravel used not only his teacher’s surname but his full name, Gabriel Fauré, turning it into a twelve-note motif.

Ravel owed much of his growth as a composer to the shelter Fauré gave him in his early years. Fauré not only stood behind him within the conservative atmosphere of the Conservatoire, but also consoled him after his repeated failures to win the Prix de Rome. Ravel, in turn, dedicated his String Quartet to Fauré as a sign of gratitude.

By 1922, Fauré, aged 77, frail and losing his hearing, had resigned as director of the Conservatoire. For his teacher in the last chapter of his life, Ravel wrote a lullaby, wishing him a peaceful close. When it was presented, Fauré was there, but he could barely hear anymore, so Ravel had to show him on the score how his name was tied to the melody. Two years later, Fauré died of pneumonia.
