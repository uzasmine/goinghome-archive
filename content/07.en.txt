The “Habanera controversy” could only make things awkward between Ravel and his mentor Debussy. Amid critics’ written quarrels over who had first worked the habanera, a Spanish dance rhythm, into piano writing, Debussy, who had never even been to Spain, won more praise, and Ravel, whose mother was of Spanish descent, could not help feeling uneasy. Indeed, “La soirée dans Grenade,” the second piece of Debussy’s Estampes, was more than enough to convey the feeling of Spain.

Debussy was a genius who could depict Spain perfectly in music from nothing more than folk melodies heard at the World’s Fair and sources found in libraries. The Spanish composer Falla praised it, saying no piece captured the atmosphere of Andalusia (southern Spain) better. Ravel probably remembered this episode for a long time.

About fifteen years later, it was Ravel’s turn to show his genius. He travelled to London to hear a performance of his Sonata for Violin and Cello, and there he met a violinist: Jelly d’Arányi, a Hungarian violinist and grandniece of the great violinist Joseph Joachim. In conversation after the concert she introduced him to Hungarian folk melodies, and on the spot he promised to write a violin piece for her.

At the time, Ravel was already planning a violin sonata for his close colleague Hélène Jourdan-Morhange. But when Jourdan-Morhange retired from performing because of a chronic illness, he set the sonata aside and decided to write a violin piece that would be Hungarian through and through. The result was Tzigane (French for “Gypsy”).

He had never been to Hungary, but that night’s conversation about folk music went on until dawn. For sources he turned to Liszt’s Hungarian Rhapsodies, and for violin technique he drew on Paganini’s Caprices with the help of his violinist colleague Jourdan-Morhange, finishing Tzigane with dazzling virtuosity pushed to the limit.

Like other Hungarian music, such as the csárdás or Liszt’s Hungarian Rhapsodies, the piece falls into a slow section (lassú) and a fast section (friss). The slow section is a violin cadenza, after which the piano joins with arpeggios in strange harmonies and the main theme is presented. As the fast section begins, the theme is varied, and every technique imaginable comes into play, from harmonics (lightly touching the string to produce thin overtones) to pizzicato with both hands (plucking the strings with the fingers instead of bowing, using not only the right hand but also the left hand that stops the strings). The music briefly grows heavier as a new syncopated theme appears in both violin and piano; its accompaniment pattern then passes to the violin, and the two repeat it, gradually speeding up, until the piece ends.

In some passages, to imitate the cimbalom, an instrument of Hungarian Roma music, the score calls for the luthéal, a new attachment at the time. Its effect was not convincing and it saw little use, so it never became widespread. Today the piece is usually played by violin and piano, and Ravel’s own orchestral version is also often performed.

Late in life, Ravel toured performing his own works and visited Hungary, where records show he conducted Tzigane along with his Piano Concerto, Boléro and Le Tombeau de Couperin. As with many beloved pieces, the critics were cold, but audiences, proud to hear their own folk music woven into an orchestral work, are said to have responded with fervent cheers and applause. To this day it remains one of the best-loved virtuoso violin works for performers and audiences alike.
