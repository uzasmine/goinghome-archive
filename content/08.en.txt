Histories of Western music usually tell the story through German and Austrian composers: from Bach to the Classical Haydn, Mozart and Beethoven, then Schubert, Schumann, Mendelssohn, Brahms, Liszt, Mahler and Strauss, all of whom worked mainly in that region. Only in the late Romantic era, Brahms’s time, did composers with distinctly national colours emerge through the national schools: Tchaikovsky in Russia, Dvořák in Bohemia, Sibelius in Finland, Grieg in Norway and Bartók in Hungary, among others.

While instrumental music developed around Germany, each country followed its own course. But when Germany, from Beethoven on, began creating instrumental music of great purity infused with literature and philosophy, Italy, the only country that could compete, was devoted to opera, and France to dance music, so their purely musical development was slow. There were no composers producing works of real stature in the genres at the heart of instrumental music, such as the sonata and the symphony. France did open up the possibility of “programme music” through Berlioz’s Symphonie fantastique, but it did not last.

On top of that, Germany produced Wagner, an opera composer like no other before or since. Beyond the music, Wagner created the very idea of staging as he realised his works, tying characters to musical motifs, the leitmotif, to express every detail of his productions. Italy kept its musical identity thanks to Verdi, born the same year as Wagner, but in France Wagner’s music became so fashionable that the country nearly found itself swallowed by German music. To break free of German influence, revive French music and protect their own tradition, French musicians led by Saint-Saëns founded the Société Nationale de Musique in 1871. Gabriel Fauré, César Franck and Jules Massenet took part, and so did Debussy.

The works of Debussy and Ravel became important enough to shift the centre of music history from Germany and Austria to France. It is no exaggeration to say that modern music began with French Impressionism.

Ravel, too, had reached the later part of his life. Unaware that the violin sonata he had written as a student would be published after his death, he set out to write what he thought was his first violin sonata. It was for his close friend, the violinist Hélène Jourdan-Morhange, and the music he loved hearing at the jazz bars he often visited became an important ingredient: another kind of music, one that had crossed over from America.

Jazz, which grew out of the work songs of American labourers, naturally had no written scores; rather than finished, organised music, it was music for jamming and improvising within certain rules. Ravel, a composer whose job was to seek out the new, not only put this material into his violin sonata but made the second movement an outright Blues. In it he imitated, in his own language, the jazz he had learned by ear in bars. It is American, yet steeped in the French colours built up through Debussy and Ravel’s own earlier works.

The first movement, in a typical sonata form, opens with a first theme played as a single line on the piano, followed by a second theme that begins with grace notes, with the motifs worked out in the development; it takes up half the total running time. The second movement is a full-fledged slow, lazy blues that captures the melancholy of a café filled with cigarette smoke. The third is a perpetuum mobile, meaning “perpetual motion.” Whether ragtime or swing, it races toward the end with its own lively momentum.

With this sonata, Western classical music absorbed yet another genre. Just as a child’s set of eight coloured pencils grows to twelve and then sixteen in the upper grades, the materials of expression grew richer. Ravel later tried to bring jazz into his piano concertos too, and the influence flowed on to Darius Milhaud in France, George Gershwin in America and Nikolai Kapustin in Russia. Jazz musicians might say it isn’t jazz, but it is at least Ravel’s own jazz, expressed in his own language.
