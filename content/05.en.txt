Called up in the First World War, Ravel served at the front as a driver, but returned to Paris earlier than expected for health reasons. Paris, however, was still within reach of the war and very dangerous. Fortunately the war ended the year after his discharge, but it left its after-effects: the mental strain of wartime trauma, depression after the death of his mother, and the death of Debussy, the older composer with whom he had a love-hate relationship.

Early in his career, Ravel was strongly influenced by Debussy. Beyond compositional technique, the two were close at first, as Debussy’s high praise for Ravel’s String Quartet shows. Later, however, the so-called “Habanera controversy” drove them apart. Rumours spread about who was imitating whom and who had plagiarised whom in bringing Spanish elements into Impressionist techniques, and episodes such as Ravel’s own timid attempt to explain himself show that their relationship was not always good.

Even so, when he heard of Debussy’s death, Ravel grieved deeply, saying he had “lost a friend.” The music magazine La Revue musicale led the publication of a tribute collection, Le Tombeau de Claude Debussy, with ten composers taking part; Ravel joined Satie, Stravinsky, Bartók, Falla and others. The piece he contributed in Debussy’s memory was the first movement of this Sonata for Violin and Cello, and a year and a half later he completed the second, third and fourth movements.

In his final years Debussy had been working on an ambitious project: six sonatas for various instruments, meant to establish a French musical tradition and identity distinct from German music. He completed only three and died with the other three still plans. For Ravel, for whom even composing had become a struggle after the war, Debussy’s death seems to have been a wake-up call. This sonata can be seen as carrying on the spirit Debussy tried to sustain to the very end.

Ravel seems to have conceived it as a sonata for violin and cello alone in order to stress the melodic independence of the two instruments. The piece also differs clearly from his earlier works in many ways. Setting harmonic colour aside, it turns to dissonance, bitonality (passages where violin and cello play in different keys) and folk-like melodies, leaning more toward modern music than Impressionism. Ravel himself called it a turning point in his career. Because he pushed in that direction deliberately, the reaction after the premiere was lukewarm, but the work has been valued more highly as time has passed.

Compared with his larger works, or chamber pieces with harp or piano, the sound itself may be lighter. But the range of tension that violin and cello create on their own is by no means small. And the meditative, deeply sorrowful mood of the slow third movement seems to hold exactly what Ravel felt after losing his mother and his mentor.
