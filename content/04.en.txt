Early summer, 1914. With the shots fired in Sarajevo, Europe was pulled into the maelstrom of war. Over four years, this tragedy changed countless lives. Artists and musicians were no exception, and neither was Ravel.

1914 is the year Ravel wrote his Piano Trio. When the First World War broke out, Ravel, expecting to be called up, was in his home region writing a piano concerto in the style of traditional Basque music. Unable to finish it, he hurried to complete a piano trio built on the same ideas. He described it himself as “five months’ work in five weeks,” cutting back on sleep to concentrate. Once mobilised and sent to the front, he could not know when he would compose again. (Fortunately, thanks to his age and slight build, he served as a driver and in supply, and was discharged relatively early.)

As his piano works Miroirs and Gaspard de la nuit already show, there is no doubt about how polished Ravel’s writing for the piano could be. The piano alone would have been enough; adding violin and cello must have been, quite literally, adding flowers to silk. If not quite an orchestra, the trio could still hold the full variety of colour he wanted to create.

The history of the piano trio goes back to Haydn, before 1800. It began with the violin doubling the melodies a piano sonata wanted to bring out, and the cello doubling the bass line. It was simple enough to be played with amateur string players. Mozart gave each instrument a little independence, and only with Beethoven did the three instruments become fully independent. Through this newly completed ensemble that Beethoven opened up, many Romantic composers were able to leave grand masterpieces that rival symphonies.

Ravel’s Piano Trio could be summed up by the old saying “learn the new by revisiting the old.” As his earlier works Gaspard de la nuit (1908), Valses nobles et sentimentales (1911) and Daphnis et Chloé (1912) show, he was already a composer who could step outside formal frameworks and use the new language of Impressionism freely. Even so, this trio follows the traditional four-movement plan of the symphony. The material that fills it, however, is entirely his own.

The first movement is in 8/8, and both the first and second themes move in a 3+2+3 pulse. This irregular rhythm reflects Basque influence. The second movement’s title, “Pantoum,” is a Malay verse form in which lines repeat in a pattern; Ravel carried this form into music by alternating and repeating two themes. The third movement, “Passacaille,” is the French name for the Baroque variation form, the passacaglia, and the fourth movement, with its irregular alternation of five and seven beats, again shows clear traces of Basque music.

History has no “what ifs,” but one can’t help wishing the war had never broken out. What would the piano concerto he was working on have been like, had he finished it? Listening to this trio, though, the regret soon fades. Of all chamber ensembles, the piano trio is the most soloistic. Each player’s skill matters, and so does balance, but what sets the trio apart from any other ensemble is the tension created when the players’ personalities and techniques collide. Debussy’s Piano Trio, like Ravel’s Violin Sonata No. 1, is a youthful work discovered long after the composer’s death. Ravel’s trio, by contrast, was written by a composer who had reached maturity in his Impressionist style. In the piano trio repertoire from the Baroque to today, it holds a central place as the representative work of Impressionism, all the more so because Ravel never wrote a symphony.
