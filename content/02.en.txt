The Classical era matters in the history of Western music because three composers, Haydn, Mozart and Beethoven, laid the groundwork for what came after. Today that fact fits neatly into one sentence, but the process can’t have been easy. Amid countless traditions and experiments, a story unfolded, and many of the forms of classical music we know today took shape. The way instruments were grouped together was one of the period’s important achievements as well.

The string quartet, a genre Haydn is said to have been the first to write for in earnest, began by chance. When Haydn was composing for Baron Karl Joseph Edler von Fürnberg at his estate, the musicians available happened to be two violinists, one violist and one cellist. From this accidental beginning, Haydn went on to write 68 string quartets and Mozart 23. Beethoven wrote 16 quartets for the same forces, plus the Grosse Fuge. Earlier, when Scarlatti wrote for the same combination, he called the works four-part sonatas and added the words “without harpsichord.” In other words, a small-ensemble sonata played without a keyboard was unusual enough that it had to be explained in advance.

Ravel was inspired by Debussy’s String Quartet to write one of his own, and in the second movement he used the same tempo marking as Debussy (Assez vif, fairly lively) as a mark of respect. He dedicated the quartet to his teacher Fauré, who was not especially fond of it, while Debussy thought highly of it. Debussy advised Ravel “not to change a single note,” yet during publication a number of notes, detailed bowings, expression marks and slurs are said to have changed.

The string quartets of the two leading Impressionist composers share some features. Both were written early in their careers. Both keep classical frameworks such as sonata form, a four-movement layout and cyclic themes. And, interestingly, neither composer wrote another string quartet. Like the late Romantics Liszt, Wagner and Mahler, both probably felt that the ensemble limited the harmonic colour and texture they wanted to create. The wineskins were too old for the new wine, so the two of them went looking for new ones.

In the early days of the string quartet, Goethe is said to have described the music like this:

> “Man hört vier vernünftige Leute sich untereinander unterhalten.”

In everyday English, that is roughly “You hear four reasonable people conversing with one another.” Yet had Goethe heard Ravel’s String Quartet, he might have described four intuitive people talking, not four reasonable ones. That is how strongly this piece stands out in the quartet repertoire for its singular colours.
